Website design for menswear stores and tailors

A menswear store's website has to answer what a customer checks before he drives over: which labels you stock, whether you make suits to measure, when the next trunk show is, and where and when to find you. Elimenti designs websites for independent menswear stores, haberdashers and custom tailors, on WordPress or custom-coded. We're in Hinsdale, IL, our founder's first website work was for a menswear boutique, and we work with stores anywhere in the US over video calls.

We started in a menswear store

Our founder's first website work, in 2025, was for Burdi Clothing, the menswear boutique on Hinsdale Avenue in Hinsdale. We still make targeted edits to Burdi's existing site, including the event pages for its Father's Day and Toys for Tots car shows. Someone else built that site. Our part is edits, and we label it that way.

Working inside it showed us how a menswear site gets used. Burdi's menu has four items: About, Location, Events and Book an Appointment. Events is the one that changes, and it always has a date attached.

For this page we also read the websites of seven other independent stores and tailors on November 18, 2026. Every example below comes from one of those sites, and all of them are linked at the bottom.

Seven pages worth having

  • Brands. A customer who wants a particular maker looks for who stocks it nearby. List your labels as text on their own page, reachable from the menu. Mr. Sid in Newton Centre, Massachusetts, has a Featured Brands page and gives makers such as Brioni a page each. Syd Jerome in Chicago puts Brands in its main menu, one page per label.
  • Made-to-measure and bespoke. Stores use these two words differently, so say what yours means: who makes the garment, how the fittings go and how to start. Rubensteins in New Orleans names the makers behind its made-to-measure program on one page, with a button to book an appointment.
  • Trunk shows and events. Give each event a page with the date, the hours, the visiting maker and how to reserve a time. The Armoury, with shops in New York and Hong Kong, keeps a trunk show calendar you can filter by category and location. It says its bespoke orders start with meeting the artisan at one of those shows, so the calendar is the front door to that work.
  • Tailoring and services. Alterations, styling appointments and wardrobe consultations are things people search for by name. Rubensteins gives on-site alterations a page of its own. Mr. Sid's services page covers made-to-measure, its tailor shop and its stylists.
  • A seasonal lookbook. A list of labels says what you stock. A lookbook shows how you'd put it together. Twice a year is enough. Mr. Sid keeps a Lookbook in its main menu.
  • Visit. Address, phone, parking and hours for every day of the week, typed out, not baked into a picture. Mr. Sid's footer carries its address, phone and daily hours on each page. Rubensteins has a page just for its free valet parking.
  • Gift cards and the shop. Link out to whatever already takes the payment. BLVDier in Chicago sends gift card buyers to a Square page. Khakis of Carmel sells its stock through a Shopify store. We don't build stores or checkout. We design the site and link it to the one you run.

For tailors, the appointment is the product

A custom tailor's site has one job: get the right person to ask for a fitting, already knowing what happens next. The sites that do this well tell him three things before he fills in the form.

The steps
Nicholas Joseph in Chicago lays its process out in three: choose the fabric and details, get fitted, receive the clothing. It offers the first appointment in its showroom or by video.
How long a visit takes
Nicholas Joseph says appointments generally last 60 to 90 minutes. Burdi's Su Misura page describes about an hour of conversation, then about thirty minutes of fitting.
How far ahead to book
BLVDier asks new clients to schedule a first fitting 12 weeks before a wedding or event. Saying so on the page spares a groom a wasted call, and spares you one too.

Then the request form can stay short: the occasion, the date the clothes are needed, and whether he's been fitted by you before. BLVDier's FAQ is sorted the way customers ask: process, weddings and events, pricing, alterations and care.

Hours and location, for customers and for Google

Google says businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show up in local search results. The details it lists include your full address and your hours, special hours included. A menswear store has more special hours than most shops: holiday weeks, trunk show evenings and days that are by appointment only.

So we put the store's name, address, phone and hours on the site as text, matching your Google Business Profile word for word. We also add them as structured data, a format search engines read directly. The rest of the local picture, including reviews, is in our guide to local SEO for a shop in the suburbs.

Photograph the floor, the bench and the staff

Catalog photos from your brands could sit on any stockist's site. Your floor, your fitting room, your tailor at the bench and your salespeople can't. Rubensteins lists its staff by name on an Our Team page. If you have strong photos, we design around them. If you don't, a shoot can go in the same proposal as the site.

Questions menswear owners ask

We already sell on Shopify. Do we need a separate website?

Not always. If your Shopify store already shows your hours, labels, services and events clearly, keep it as it is. A separate site makes sense when the shop floor is the business and online orders are a side door. Then the site is built around the store, its tailoring and its events, with a link to the Shopify store for orders. We don't build online stores.

Who adds trunk show dates and new labels?

On a WordPress site, you can, with your own login and a walkthrough at launch. Or send the details to Site Care and the change is live within five business days. For a store with a full events calendar, we usually recommend WordPress for that reason.

Can you work on the site we have without replacing it?

If it runs on WordPress, yes. That's the arrangement with Burdi: targeted edits to its existing site, billed by the hour. If the site is on another platform, or every edit breaks something, read about a website redesign.

Do you work with stores outside Illinois?

Yes. Every project runs over video calls, from the first conversation to launch, so the store can be anywhere in the US.
