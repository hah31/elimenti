What WordPress maintenance costs, and what each price gets you

Published WordPress maintenance pricing runs from about $30 a month for automated updates to several thousand dollars a month for plans with hosting, testing and reserved developer hours. For a single business site, the plans below run from $89 a month at WP Buffs to $435 at SiteCare (hosting included), and hourly help runs from $15 to $28 an hour on Upwork to $80 to $120 an hour on Codeable. Here's what moves the price, what plans leave out and what to ask before you sign.

By Hashim H., Elimenti. Published November 25, 2026.

The short answer

Every WordPress site needs the same basic upkeep: WordPress itself, the theme and every plugin get updated, and there's a recent backup to restore if an update breaks something. Software can do most of that on its own, which is why the cheapest plans cost little. Codeable's 2026 pricing guide puts the whole market at $30 to $5,000-plus a month, and says the gap between a $50 plan and a $240 one comes down to human involvement.

What you pay more for is a person. Someone who tests updates on a copy of the site before they go live, fixes what breaks, makes your edits and answers when you call. Hosting bundled into the plan, an online store and a site with many plugins or page builders all push the price up.

The figures in this guide are other providers' published prices, with sources. Elimenti's own WordPress maintenance is scoped to each site; how it works is at the end.

Paying by the hour or by the task

Not every site needs a monthly plan. A site that rarely changes can be cared for by the hour, as long as someone remembers to run the updates.

  • Freelance marketplaces. Upwork's rate page lists WordPress developers at $15 to $28 an hour. Codeable lists $80 to $120 an hour for its developers.
  • One-off emergencies. The same guide puts recovering a hacked site at $200 to $2,000-plus. Some plans include cleanup; Codeable's Basic plan quotes it separately, and its Advanced plan includes it.

Hourly care costs less in a quiet year and more in a busy one. If you pay by the hour, ask your developer to tell you when updates are waiting, so they don't get skipped.

Doing it yourself

WordPress can install its own updates, and plugins and themes can be set to update automatically one by one. ManageWP's free dashboard manages updates across sites and includes a monthly cloud backup. A dedicated backup service such as Jetpack VaultPress Backup adds real-time backups and one-click restores, at the list price above.

The tools are cheap. What they don't do is look at the site afterward: an update can stop a contact form from sending, and nothing tells you until a customer does. If you do it yourself, back up before every round of updates, then check your key pages and send yourself a test inquiry.

What plans include, and what costs extra

Most plans, at any price, cover:

  • Updates to WordPress, the theme and the plugins.
  • Backups, kept somewhere other than your server.
  • Uptime monitoring, so someone hears when the site goes down.
  • Security or malware scanning, on many plans.

Often extra, or left to you:

  • Premium plugin and theme licenses. Paid plugins renew every year in your name. Elementor's single-site Pro plans list at $60 or $84 a year and renew at the same price. Codeable's guide puts a site's premium licenses at $200 to $1,000 a year.
  • Hosting. Some plans bundle it, like SiteCare's. Most don't, and Codeable's guide puts managed WordPress hosting at $30 to $100 a month. Compare plans with hosting against plans without it plus the cost of your host.
  • Edits beyond the allowance. Plans that include edits define them: a number a month, small tasks only or one request at a time. "Unlimited" means different things at different companies, so ask what counts.
  • An online store. WooCommerce sites need more testing because checkout has to keep working. Codeable's guide puts WooCommerce maintenance at $500 to $3,000-plus a month.
  • Redesigns and new features. Maintenance keeps the site you have working. New layouts and features are quoted as projects.

What drives the price for your site

  • How many plugins and builders it runs. Every plugin is another update, and page builders add more pieces that can shift after one. ABN Construction Group's site in Lombard, which we care for, runs two page builders, Elementor and Neuron, plus a slider plugin and an SEO plugin.
  • Whether updates are tested first. Updating a staging copy and checking it before touching the live site takes longer than clicking "update all", and it's what keeps a busy season from starting with a broken site.
  • How much change the site sees. A site with new events, listings or pages most months needs edit time built in. A brochure site that changes twice a year doesn't.

Red flags in a maintenance plan

  • Updates with no backup first. WordPress's own documentation says to have a backup before you update.
  • "Update all" and done. Nobody opens the site afterward to check the forms, the menus and the pages that bring in business.
  • No record of what changed. If something breaks next month, a log of which updates ran and when is how it gets found quickly.
  • Logins and licenses in the provider's name. Your hosting, domain, administrator account and premium licenses should be yours, so you can switch providers without starting over.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What exactly do you update, how often, and do you test updates before they go live?
  • Where are backups kept, for how long, and when did you last restore one?
  • How many edits or hours are included, and how is extra work priced?
  • How fast do you respond, and what counts as an emergency?
  • Is hosting included? Are premium plugin licenses?
  • Whose name are the logins and licenses in?
  • If we stop working together, what do I get back?

Local or remote, for a Chicago-suburbs business

Most maintenance services work remotely, and that works: a website lives on a server, not in an office. What a local provider adds is a person you can sit down with, who knows the towns and the customers you sell to. For a business in Hinsdale or anywhere in DuPage County, ask a local provider and a national one the same questions above. The answers matter more than the address.

Common questions

How much does WordPress maintenance cost per month?

Published plans for a single site run from $89 a month at WP Buffs to $240 to $280 at Codeable and $435 at SiteCare Gold, which includes hosting. Codeable's 2026 guide puts the whole market at $30 to $5,000-plus a month. The price follows how much developer time, testing and hosting the plan includes.

Can I maintain a WordPress site myself?

Yes. WordPress can update itself, ManageWP's free dashboard manages updates and a monthly cloud backup, and a backup service like Jetpack VaultPress Backup lists at $9.95 a month, billed yearly. What you give up is someone checking the site after each update and fixing what an update breaks.

Does a page builder like Elementor change the cost?

It can. Elementor's paid plans are a yearly license, listed at $60 or $84 a year for one site and renewing at the same price, and a page-builder site has more pieces to check after each update. A site with two builders, like the one we care for at ABN Construction Group, takes longer to learn before anything changes.

How does Elimenti price WordPress maintenance?
