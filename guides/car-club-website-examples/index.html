Car club website examples: what real club sites get right

The best car club websites answer three questions quickly: what the club is, where the next drive or meet is, and how someone gets in. Below are real club sites, from national marque clubs to local chapters to private, invitation-based clubs, with what each one does well and one thing it misses. Every site was checked on November 5, 2026.

By Hashim H., Elimenti. Published November 5, 2026.

Who's looking at these

I co-founded Red Devils Garage, a local car club, and organized its charity car meet in the Hinsdale Central High School parking lot. More than 200 cars came out, and it raised $10,000 for HCS Family Services. At Elimenti, we also designed and custom-coded the website for 1 of 1 Car Club, a private Porsche club. So I read club sites two ways: as the person trying to get a meet in front of people, and as the person deciding what goes on each page.

The clubs below aren't ranked. They're grouped by the kind of club, because a national marque club and a small invitation-only club need different things from a website.

National marque clubs

Big clubs devoted to one make, usually with a magazine, local regions and decades of history. Their sites carry the most, so organization matters more than looks.

Porsche Club of America

pca.org

What it does well: It makes a national club feel local. "From the Regions," a roundup of what members are doing in their own regions, runs monthly in the club's magazine, Panorama, and weekly on the website, where there's no limit on space. The latest Panorama is linked from a bar at the top of the homepage, and Classifieds and Technical sit in the main menu next to Membership and Events.

What it misses: On a phone, a newsletter sign-up box and the cookie notice covered most of the first screen before we'd seen anything of the club. A newcomer's first impression is two things to close.

Cadillac & LaSalle Club

cadillaclasalleclub.org

What it does well: The first paragraph tells a prospective member everything that matters: an international club of more than 6,200 members with over 12,000 collectible cars, covering every Cadillac from the single-cylinder models to the V-16s, open to anyone, with no need to own a Cadillac or LaSalle. Members get their own section for back issues of the club's magazine, The Self-Starter, for renewing and updating the list of cars they own, and for photo albums of past events.

What it misses: The office hours disagree with each other. The bar at the top of the page says Monday to Friday, 9 to 5 Central; the footer says Monday to Thursday, with Friday closed. A member planning to call on a Friday can't tell which is right.

Shelby American Automobile Club

saac.com

What it does well: One sentence does the work of an About page. The club is dedicated to "the world championship cars from Shelby American, and the cars they have inspired from 1962 to today," and you don't have to own one to join: "just enthusiasm." The menu shows what a marque club with history runs on: Registry, Regions, Conventions, Concours, Magazine and Forum. A wide photo of red Shelby Mustangs does the rest.

What it misses: The homepage's upcoming events are two lines, "MCACN November 21" and "SAAC-52 July 15," with no place, no year and nothing to tell a newcomer what MCACN is.

Local and regional clubs

Smaller clubs live on turnout. Their sites have one main job: getting the next person to show up.

Windy City BMW Chapter, BMW Car Club of America

windycitybmw.org

What it does well: The homepage reads like an active club. Upcoming events have dates, times and places, like an open house at URW Automotive in Crystal Lake on November 7 and the annual dinner at Double Clutch in Evanston on January 9. Below them are short write-ups of recent outings: a fall tour that ended at Starved Rock, the season's last autocross, cars and coffee in St. Charles. The current newsletter sits in the sidebar. Anyone deciding whether the chapter is worth joining can see it's busy.

What it misses: The footer still reads "© 2017." Everything above it is current, but a careful visitor notices an old year in the footer, and it says the opposite of the rest of the page.

Chicago Corvette Club

chicagocorvetteclub.com

What it does well: It tells a newcomer exactly where to show up: the third Wednesday of the month, dinner at 6:30 and the business meeting at 7:30, at Oak Street Restaurant and Bar in North Aurora. It invites people to a meeting or event before asking them to join, the homepage leads with a photo of members and their cars, and the About page tells the founding story, starting with 22 Corvette enthusiasts meeting at a Chevrolet dealership on March 21, 1958.

What it misses: The Become a Member link leads to a paragraph on why to join, not how. Neither the homepage nor the About page says what membership costs or how to sign up, and the only next step offered is the club's email address.

Private and invitation-based clubs

These clubs aren't trying to sign up everyone. Their sites have to make the right people want in, and make the way in clear.

Fast Lane Drive

fastlanedrive.com

What it does well: Two short paragraphs explain how membership actually works. You apply, you're invited into a chapter, and from then on you can attend events at any chapter. Each chapter runs a monthly drive, usually a rally on scenic roads followed by dinner or a night out. All 33 chapters are listed by city, Chicago included, and the next few events are on the homepage with dates.

What it misses: The page pulls in two directions. The headline calls it "the world's most exclusive exotic car club," and a few lines later it's "the world's largest members-only exotic car club," and a counter further down shows 2,000-plus entrepreneurs and executives. For a club that screens its members, the size claim works against the exclusivity one.

Supercar Owners Circle

supercarownerscircle.com

What it does well: The design suits the members it wants: dark, quiet and photo-led, with "Join the Experience" and "Member Log In" side by side on the first screen. Five plain headings, events, services, access, privileges and networking, say what membership gets you, and an events gallery going back to 2024 shows the club has been doing this for years.

What it misses: Under "Upcoming events," the two events listed were dated April 23 and June 26, 2026, both past when we looked in November. Past dates in an upcoming list make an active club look idle.

1 of 1 Car Club

Our case study

A private, invitation-only Porsche club based in Chicago and Dallas, built around its founder's private collection. We designed and custom-coded its site, so read this one knowing that.

What it does well: The site is about the cars and the club, not the founder, whose name isn't on it. The Collection page reads every car from a single data file, so adding one means filling in one entry and dropping photos in a folder, and a car with no photos yet shows its year in large type with the model beneath it, instead of a gray box. The application asks for name, city, the Porsches someone owns and why they want in, and sends it straight to the club's inbox, so the questions do the first round of screening.

What it misses: Its four pages, Home, Philosophy, The Collection and Ownership, don't include an events page, so the site doesn't tell a member where the club meets next. The site is shown in the case study as we delivered it in 2026; the domain, 1of1carclub.com, now carries a different site.

What the good ones have in common

  • They say who the club is for in the first paragraph, including whether you have to own the car. The Cadillac & LaSalle Club and the Shelby club both answer that before you scroll.
  • They tell people where to show up. A day, a time and an address do more than "join us at an upcoming event."
  • They keep the event list honest. Dates, places and times for what's next, and past events turned into a short write-up with photos, the way Windy City BMW does it.
  • They explain how someone gets in. For an open club, the dues and a form. For a private one, an application that asks the right questions and says what happens after it's sent.
  • They make a big club feel local. Region and chapter news, like PCA's weekly roundup, shows members what's happening near them.
  • They get the small things right. Office hours that match, a current year in the footer and nothing covering the first screen on a phone. Visitors notice these before they notice the design.

How we looked at these sites

We opened each club's homepage on November 5, 2026, on a desktop screen and as a phone 390 pixels wide, and checked the Chicago Corvette Club's About page for how to join. Everything above is what those public pages showed that day. Members-only areas weren't seen, and sites change. None of these clubs is a client except 1 of 1 Car Club, and none of them asked to be included.

Building a site for your club?

What the site needs depends on the kind of club you run.

  • A modern or private club. Applications, member cars and keeping the right things private: see car club website design.
  • A classic or marque club with a long history. Chapters, a magazine and newsletter archive, a member directory, events, and moving an old site to WordPress so volunteers can edit it: see websites for classic and marque car clubs.
