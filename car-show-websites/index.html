Car show and concours website design

A car show website has two readers before the day: the owner deciding whether to enter a car, and the spectator deciding whether to come. After the day it has a third job, keeping the results and photos where people can find them next year. Elimenti designs websites for car shows, concours d'elegance, cars-and-coffee series and charity car meets anywhere in the US, on WordPress so volunteers can update them, or custom-coded.

What a show's site has to do

  • Fill the field. Classes, who can enter, the deadline and one clear way to register.
  • Bring people to the gate. Date, hours, address, parking and admission, easy to read on a phone in the car.
  • Give sponsors a place. This year's sponsors thanked by name, and next year's told how to sign on.
  • Name the cause. Which charity benefits, and what the show has given so far.
  • Hold the record. Winners, photos and past years, still online after the tents come down.

The entry page, for owners

An owner opens the entry page with one question: is my car right for this show, and what do I do next? The page should settle it without an email to the committee.

  • Classes, with years. Each class named with the years or makes it covers, worded the same on every page that mentions it.
  • How cars get in. Open registration, or nomination and a selection committee. If cars are selected, say when owners will hear back.
  • What to have ready. Photos, the car's details and its story, listed before the form starts, so nobody quits halfway to go find a VIN.
  • The show day. When check-in opens, when cars must be parked, when judging starts, and the field rules: fire extinguishers, wheel chocks, chairs and tents.

We don't build payment or ticketing systems. Entry fees, tickets and donations stay in the registration or ticketing tool the event already uses, and the site sends people to the right page there at the right moment. Burdi Clothing's Father's Day car show page works this way: its first line links to the show's Eventbrite listing, where owners register and anyone can donate.

The spectator page

Most visitors only want to know whether to come and how to get there.

  • When. The day, the date and the hours the public is welcome, which usually differ from when show cars arrive.
  • Where. A street address that opens in a maps app, plus the entrance to use.
  • Parking. Which lot is for spectators, what it costs, and where accessible parking and any shuttle are.
  • Admission. Free, at the gate or in advance. If tickets are sold ahead, the button goes to the ticket seller.
  • What to leave at home. Pets, drones, coolers, pop-up tents: whatever the venue doesn't allow.

All of it belongs on the page as words. A flyer image alone hides those details from screen readers and is hard to read on a phone.

Sponsors and the cause

A sponsor page does two jobs. It thanks this year's sponsors by name, each with a link. And it tells a business thinking about next year who attends, what a sponsor gets and who to call. The full detail can live in a sponsor prospectus, the short PDF the committee already emails around. The page only has to say enough that a business owner asks for it.

If the show raises money, the beneficiary goes on the homepage by name, with what the show has given so far. Entrants, spectators and sponsors all look for it.

When the plan changes

Weather moves car shows. The site needs one obvious spot for a notice that says the show is on, delayed or moved to its rain date, with the time the notice went up. Someone at the field has to be able to post it on the morning, without calling a web designer.

That shapes the build. For most shows we'd recommend WordPress, so a volunteer with a login can change the notice, the schedule or a parking detail themselves. On a custom-coded site every change comes through us, and Site Care turns changes around in five business days. That suits an archive, not a rain call.

After the show: results, photos and the archive

The week after, people come back for two things: who won, and where the photos are.

  • Results by class. Car, owner and award, typed out as text, so an owner searching their own name finds the page.
  • Photos. A gallery on the site, or a link to the photographer's own gallery, with a credit.
  • A page for each year. Don't wipe the site to announce the next show. Move this year's classes, results and sponsors to a page of their own and keep it.
  • Next year's date. One line on the homepage as soon as it's set, even if nothing else is ready.

Vendors, volunteers and press

Three smaller groups each need one page.

  • Vendors. What a space includes, the deadline and how to apply.
  • Volunteers. The jobs, the shifts and a sign-up, which can be the sign-up tool you already use.
  • Press. Who to contact, how to request a pass and a few photos they're free to run.

A monthly meet is a different site

A cars-and-coffee series has no classes and no judges. It has a calendar and a parking lot. The first screen should give the next date, the hours and the address. Below that go the rules that keep the venue: where show cars park, where spectators park, when the lot opens, and no revving or burnouts on the way out. Short posts from past mornings, with photos, show a newcomer what turns up.

Questions organizers ask

Can people register and pay through the site?

They register through the service you already use, and the site links to it. We don't build payment, ticketing or store systems. If you take entries through a registration service, a ticketing site or a form, each button goes straight to the right page there, and the money stays where your treasurer already tracks it.

Can our volunteers update the site themselves?

On WordPress, yes. Each volunteer who needs one gets a login, and at launch we walk them through the pages they'll change most: the schedule, the notice, sponsors, results and photos.

What happens to the site after the show?

It stays up. This year's schedule, results, photos and sponsors move to a page for that year, and the homepage turns to next year's date as soon as you have one. Past years stay online as the show's record.

Can you work on the site we already have?

Often, yes. If the current site works and needs event pages written, results posted or details corrected, that's edit work through Site Care, not a rebuild. It's what we do for Burdi Clothing's car show pages.

When should we start, relative to the show date?

Count back from the day entries open, not from the show. Most of our projects launch four to six weeks after kickoff, and content sets the pace: classes, the schedule, sponsor logos and photos from past years.

Do you work with shows outside Illinois?

Yes. We're based in Hinsdale, Illinois, and every project runs over video calls, so we take events anywhere in the US.
