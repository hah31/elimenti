Classic car restoration shop website design

A restoration shop's website should show each build in the order it happened, state plainly which cars the shop takes and give an owner an easy way to send photos of theirs. Elimenti designs websites for classic car restoration shops, restomod and hot rod builders and marque specialists across the US, built on WordPress so the shop can post its own updates, or custom-coded. No restoration shop is on our client list yet. The nearest project is 1 of 1 Car Club's site, which we built for a private Porsche club.

What an owner looks for before calling

  • Cars like theirs. The makes and eras you know, with finished examples.
  • Work done under your roof. Which trades are in-house and which go to a partner.
  • How they'll be kept informed. Road Scholars, a Porsche specialist in Durham, North Carolina, says on its site that clients get monthly progress reports with photographs, and a binder documenting the car and its restoration when the job is done.
  • Honest timing. How long builds run and when you could start.
  • Results someone else judged. Show awards and magazine features, each tied to a car.

A page for every build

A finished car hides most of the labor that went into it. The build page shows that labor, stage by stage:

  • As it arrived. The car on the trailer, and what the owner asked for.
  • Teardown. What came apart and what you found underneath.
  • Metalwork. Rust cut out, panels made, gaps set, shown in bare steel.
  • Paint. Primer, blocking, color and the first time it rolled into daylight.
  • Assembly. Drivetrain, wiring, glass, trim and interior going back in.
  • Delivered. Finished photos, and any trophy it has won since.

Every car is a single entry carrying a status. Road Scholars puts one on each restoration in its list and gives the car its own page of specifications and history. Classic Car Studio in Missouri lets visitors filter projects by make and by type, including full builds still in progress. On WordPress, adding a stage means uploading that week's photos and writing three lines.

Before-and-after galleries that load on a phone

One restoration can produce thousands of photos, and plenty of people will open them on a phone.

  • Sized for the screen. We save every picture at several sizes, so a phone never downloads the desktop file.
  • Loaded as you scroll. The first row shows at once, and the others wait until they're needed.
  • Before and after, side by side. Same angle, same crop, one caption.
  • Captions that say something. "New lower quarter, formed by hand" beats a file name.
  • No autoplay slideshow. People want to stop on the weld.

Services, split by discipline

Many owners arrive needing one trade, not a full restoration. Several of the shops we studied sort their service menus by discipline. We'd give each one its own page, linked to builds where that trade shows:

  • Metal and body. Rust repair, panel fabrication and fit.
  • Paint. Prep, color and finish, with the paint system named.
  • Upholstery and trim. Seats, tops, carpets and brightwork.
  • Mechanical. Engine, gearbox, suspension and brakes.
  • Electrical. Rewiring, gauges and conversions.
  • Concours preparation. Detailing and correctness work ahead of a judged show.

Say what you take, and how long the wait is

Paul Russell and Company's homepage names its field right at the top: pre-war through 1960s Mercedes-Benz, Ferrari, Porsche, Bugatti and Alfa Romeo. A reader knows within seconds whether to keep going. State yours as exactly, whether it's first-generation Camaros, air-cooled Porsches or prewar Fords.

Then name the jobs you turn down: collision repair, a project another shop took apart, single small repairs. A line such as "booking metal and paint for next fall" spares both sides a call, provided someone keeps it current. We'd store it in one place, so a single edit changes it everywhere.

An inquiry form that asks for photos

Classic Car Studio's consultation form asks for year, make, model, service type and a budget range, and takes photo uploads. It also prints typical costs: most factory-correct rotisserie restorations at $100,000 to $150,000, and most full custom builds from $250,000 (their figures, checked November 18, 2026). Whether or not you publish numbers, ask for:

  • The car. Year, make, model and the state it's sitting in.
  • Its condition. Running or not, complete or missing parts, known rust, already apart.
  • The goal. A reliable driver, local shows, concours judging or a restomod.
  • Photos. All four sides, the trouble spots and the underside if they can.
  • Timing. When they'd like to begin.

Uploads land in your inbox, and the thank-you page tells the owner when to expect a reply.

Awards, press and video

List awards by show and year, and link each to the build page of the car that won. Paul Russell's awards page groups its wins by concours with the cars named beneath. Link magazine features to the original article.

Video belongs on build pages too: a shop tour, a first start, a walkaround. We embed it from YouTube or Vimeo so it loads only once someone presses play.

Cars and parts for sale

We don't build online stores or checkout. If you sell cars through an auction site or a dealer listing, or parts through a store you already run, each item on your website links to wherever it's listed. When it sells, it moves to a sold archive. That archive is worth keeping, because it records what has passed through the shop.

Clients stay private unless they agree

Owner names, home towns, plates and full chassis numbers stay off build pages unless the owner agrees in writing. Some owners want the credit, and a car with real history may need its chassis number told. Many don't. We also remove location data from every photo, so a shot taken in a client's driveway doesn't reveal the address.

Questions shop owners ask

Can we post build updates ourselves?

Yes, on WordPress. Whoever takes the photos gets a login, and at launch we walk them through adding a stage to a build: upload, caption, publish. If nobody at the shop has time, Site Care posts updates for you within five business days of receiving the photos.

Can an owner follow their own build privately?

Yes. WordPress can put any page behind a password, so only someone holding the password can open a client's build page while the work is underway. It goes public only if they agree once the car is done.

Should a restoration shop publish prices?

Publish whatever you can stand behind. That might be your shop rate, a typical range for a common job or a plain statement that you can't quote before inspecting the car, along with what the inspection involves. Any of the three tells an owner more than silence does.

We already have a WordPress site. Can you improve it instead of rebuilding?

Often, yes. Site Care covers existing WordPress sites built by someone else, which is the work we do for ABN Construction Group. We go through the theme and plugins first, then estimate each task. If the site is too fragile to keep editing, we'll tell you.

Do you work with shops outside Illinois?

Yes. Elimenti is in Hinsdale, Illinois, and takes shops in any state, with meetings held by video. Photos from the shop floor are fine for build stages. For finished cars, we work with images from a photographer near you.

How pricing works
